Office Politics, Decoded

Mike Lao Li on being heard, protecting your work, and growing through reorgs in English-speaking workplaces

41 of 41 transcripts included; 0 excluded; 0 pending.

Mike Lao Li (麦克老李) spent about a decade at Amazon, rising from warehouse front-line manager to L8 director and general manager, before starting his own business teaching workplace communication. His audience is mostly Chinese and other East Asian professionals working in English. His through-line is that office politics is not a dark art reserved for schemers: it is how people with limited time and incomplete information decide whom to trust, and a few habits from school and home quietly send the wrong signals.

The book moves from mindset to tactics to the bigger picture. Part 1 names the habits that read differently at work. Parts 2 and 3 cover meetings, blame, and workload, with scripts you can use the next day. Part 4 follows a manager and a former report rebuilding trust from both sides. Part 5 turns to reorgs, when trust becomes scope. Part 6 collects follower questions about humor, small talk, disagreement, and credit. Part 7 steps outside the channel to an Amazon vice president's frank account of executive politics and to notes on The Manager's Path.

Part 1. See the game: habits that read differently at work

Mike's recurring claim is that many East Asian professionals lose ground in English-speaking workplaces through habits that were virtues at school or at home: restating what the teacher said, waiting for the perfect answer, apologizing to keep the peace, and letting good work speak for itself. These sections name those habits, explain how a senior manager reads them, and offer replacements. Start here, because most tactics in later parts assume this shift in mindset.

Section 01 · lesson

Treat your career like a product: skill times self-marketing

Mike replaces the Chinese question of whether to focus on people or on work with a product model: career success is expertise multiplied by self-marketing, limited by a fixed budget of time and energy.

Viewers often ask Mike whether being good with people or being good at the work matters more. He says that question belongs to a Chinese workplace, where his own experience is a two-and-a-half-month college internship, and that forcing it onto an English-speaking workplace is awkward. His alternative is to productize yourself. An iPhone needs both a strong product and strong marketing: excellent features without marketing do not sell, and great marketing cannot save an obsolete product.

A clear goal removes noise

Mike treats total time as skill time plus marketing time plus time spent handling noise. Noise returns nothing, so the first move is to shrink it, and only a clear goal lets you see what noise is. Before 2017 he had no clear goal and followed rules his parents gave him about getting along with everyone. In 2017 he set a goal of reaching senior manager within 24 to 36 months. Showing his expertise in meetings and claiming the credit he had earned became the priority; whether colleagues thought he was bragging became noise he could decide to ignore.

Source: Noise and the 2017 goal ↗

Inherited rules inflate the noise

He argues that much internal friction comes from goals handed to us by parents, teachers, and society. The same voices tell us to tend every relationship along the way and warn us not to self-promote, with sayings such as good wine needs no bush and gold will always shine. In his reading, that advice sets three of the four variables against you: a goal that is not yours, a larger share of noise, and a smaller share of marketing. Systems everywhere are designed to run at the lowest cost, he says, and your success is not part of their formula.

Source: Inherited rules ↗

The right ratio depends on level

There is no golden ratio of skill to marketing; it depends on level and job. A new hire should spend most time on skill but can stand out with only a little marketing, because most people do none. Mike cites a figure he describes as real data: 97 to 99 percent of LinkedIn users never post. More important, though, is self-promotion inside the company, in meetings in front of your manager and skip-level manager. For directors and vice presidents, selling the vision repeatedly is part of the job, and in customer-facing roles marketing yourself, your team, and your product becomes a large share of the work.

Source: Ratio by level and role ↗

Mike's first mistake as a director

After his promotion to director he kept working with a senior manager's mindset and did not sell his vision or his team. Within a few months that caused problems. Two vice presidents he knew told him, separately and in similar words, that a director's biggest job is to sell the vision, the service, and the team to everyone he talks to. He increased the marketing share and gradually turned the situation around.

Source: Selling the vision as a director ↗

Takeaways

  • Write down a concrete career goal and a time frame; use it to label noise you can stop managing.
  • Do at least some visible self-promotion, especially in meetings with your manager and skip-level manager.
  • Expect the marketing share to grow as your level grows; at director level, selling the vision is the job.

Section 02 · lesson

Recap less, synthesize more: drop the student habit

Restating what your manager just said feels respectful and careful, but at work it doubles the manager's mental load; summarizing to move the group forward, or turning instructions into actions, earns trust instead.

When a manager finishes speaking, many of us repeat our understanding back to confirm it. Mike says that can please a teacher, but at work it adds to the manager's cognitive load and slowly moves you away from their trust.

Source: The recap habit ↗

Two costs of the recap habit

  • Loss of efficiency and momentum. It interrupts the flow of a conversation in a way normal talk never does. If someone asks what food you like and you say beef noodles and salt-crisp chicken, a reply of let me confirm, you like beef noodles and salt-crisp chicken, is that right? would leave you puzzled.
  • Perceived misalignment of effort. The manager has already answered; now they must listen again and judge whether your restatement is correct. Understanding was your job, and the recap hands it back to them, so they are doing two people's work.
Source: Two harms ↗

Recap versus synthesis

Mike anticipates an objection: he has elsewhere told viewers to summarize in meetings. The difference is purpose. Around 2017, on a small launch, he worked with someone he calls Xiao T, who recapped every statement to confirm his own understanding, and someone he calls Xiao S, who synthesized. After a long, scattered discussion Xiao S would pause the group: so far we have identified three root causes, these are the priorities, the goal is a quick fix for the most visible symptom, so can we go deeper on option one and test its viability? Xiao T centered his own understanding; Xiao S centered the project and steered everyone toward a direction.

Source: Xiao T and Xiao S ↗

He acknowledges the good intent behind recapping: we want to be sure we match what the boss wants and avoid execution mistakes, we need to run the request through our own problem-solving frameworks before it becomes an action, and we were raised to show the speaker we are listening. The intention is fine; the way it sounds is the problem.

Section 03 · lesson

Swap exam thinking for the 90-60-10 rule

Mike argues that exam training teaches a predict-and-simulate habit that freezes us in meetings; most remarks can be reversed, so speak in feedback mode and save full simulation for the few conversations that truly matter.

Why exam thinking stalls you

Mike watched a video of a middle-school teacher solving a shaded-area geometry problem by drawing an auxiliary line, rotating and moving shapes until the answer appeared. He could not solve it himself, but it made him notice something: an exam gives exactly the conditions you need, has one correct answer, and has an examiner. A meeting has none of those. Ten equally smart people are present, conditions are never exactly sufficient, and everyone is both solving and setting problems. Running exam-style simulations in that setting overloads us, and we wait for the moment our answer is fully confirmed. That moment never comes, so we fall a step behind and stay behind.

Source: Exam thinking in meetings

He contrasts our predict-and-simulate mode with the feedback mode he sees in many Western colleagues: say it, watch the reaction, adjust if someone objects, and treat silence as agreement. Around 2015 his boss told him he was very quiet in meetings and needed to be more engaged. Mike felt wronged because his mind was fully engaged. Later the boss explained what he saw: silence reads as no interest or no leadership, and once a line like he does not seem to have an opinion reaches your boss, it can damage your review and promotion.

Source: Feedback mode and the 2015 feedback

The fix his boss pointed to was Amazon's bias-for-action principle: speed matters, and many decisions are reversible and do not need extensive analysis. Mike says he often fell into analysis paralysis. If a decision can be reversed, roughly 60 percent of the data is enough; waiting for 100 percent means the moment has passed. He recommends the book Working Backwards, written by two former Amazon senior executives, for its decision-making mental models, while noting that bureaucracy can distort these principles in a large company and that bias for action can carry other meanings in office politics that he leaves for another time.

Source: Bias for action and 60 percent data

Phrases that keep you safe

For gaining visibility, Mike suggests wrapping an idea in a question rather than a statement: have we thought about this aspect of the business, or this risk? If the answer is yes, a quick just wanted to double-check closes it; if no, you can offer maybe we could do this. He calls this a fake question and says that in leadership training they do not recommend it for leading a team, but for an individual trying to be seen it is useful. When a statement goes wrong, back-paddle without treating it as an admission: let me revise that, I will take that back and say what I actually meant, or, as he sometimes says to VPs, I misspoke on that, let me elaborate. It is simply thinking out loud.

Source: Questions and back-paddling

Keep the simulation brain

Mike does not want viewers to discard the predict-and-simulate habit. It is the right tool for strategy, reorganizations, and staffing decisions, and when it shows in English it reads as greater wisdom. The mistake is using it everywhere, rewriting a Slack message three times or rehearsing every sentence: a cannon aimed at a fly.

Source: Where simulation belongs · Do not throw it away

Stop over-apologizing and making goodwill promises

Saying sorry and promising to try as hard as you can are meant to show respect and calm things down, but senior leaders hear weakness, no real commitment, and a story in which everything was your fault.

Mike's test: count how often you say I am sorry in a day. More than twice is probably overuse. Early in his career he apologized in almost every other sentence when asking a question, and after a mistake he would add I will try as hard as I can. He calls these goodwill actions, and in a senior leader's ears a goodwill action means no action: I will try as hard as I can sounds like you will not really try. The intent is respect and de-escalation, avoiding conflict and blame.

Source: Symptoms and intent

How excessive apology is perceived

  • It dilutes meaning and authenticity, and overuse leads to distrust.
  • It suggests a competence gap. There is already a power imbalance when you apologize to a boss or stakeholder; over-apologizing tilts it further until you lose respect.
  • It creates a logical conflict Mike only felt after being promoted: if you had really tried as hard as you said, why did the problem happen?
  • Blaming yourself for everything implies the whole failure was disproportionately yours, when many factors were usually involved. Promise to try harder, and if the result still disappoints, those unspoken factors land on you.
Source: Four perceptions

Did you actually miss it?

He starts with mindset. First ask whether you really missed something, or whether other factors only make it look like your fault. If it is not your fault, apologizing is another form of arrogance: saying something for surface harmony. Second, there may be a bigger game behind the curtain. Admitting fault in a meeting can force your boss to do cleanup work you cannot see, so ask whether your boss needs you to hold the line.

Source: Mindset shift
Editorial synthesis
Source: Owning a real miss

Early in his career, after a miss he would feel ashamed, replay how he could have missed it, and mentally check out of the rest of the meeting, even though he might have had useful input on the real business problem. The leaders he saw earn trust from VPs and senior VPs talked about the business even when their own team had made the mistake.

Source: Executive presence after a miss

Caveat

None of this means being a jerk. The goal is solving a business problem, so do not let emotion push you to either extreme, over-apologetic or harsh.

Source: Do not overcorrect

Section 06 · lesson

Compliment less to be trusted more

Heavy praise for a senior person is meant to build rapport quickly, but it signals that you need something and puts you below them; trust starts from level ground.

Mike's rule of thumb: using more than five words to tell someone how great they are counts as over-praising. I have heard so many great things about you, followed by more, is too much; I like your shirt is fine. The urge is strongest with someone senior or someone new, and Mike says he still catches himself doing it. The intent is respect and quick rapport in a foreign language and culture, but it backfires. It raises doubts about competence, which creates a power imbalance and costs respect. And, as in our own culture, excessive compliments make the listener wonder what you want from them, which grows into skepticism and distrust if it becomes a pattern.

Source: Over-praise and its effects

Decoding vague feedback

He adds an aside about vague feedback such as you need to be more assertive, more confident, or improve your communication. Bosses often give it because they were never trained to give feedback. Leading, managing, and coaching are separate skills; many bosses master one, and coaching is the hardest. You hear what you are not, but not how to change.

Source: Why feedback is vague

The fix starts with the idea that trust requires two people on level ground with mutual respect. People sense friendliness without extra words; humans are wired to read friend or foe quickly. They also sense, just as quickly, that you need something from them. So reverse-engineer it: go into the conversation with the mindset that you need nothing from this person. You will feel less needy, and the rest follows. Less is more, with the same caveat as before: you do not need to become cold, unsmiling, or aggressive, just rebalance the urge to be friendly.

Source: Level ground and needing nothing

Section 07 · lesson

Lead with the point: too much context costs you the seat

Long context before the point is meant to be thorough and respectful, but leaders hear someone who does not know the priority, may be hiding something, and is overloading them.

The third politeness trap Mike describes is giving too much context before making the point or asking the question. The intent is thoroughness, respect, and a worry about being misunderstood. As he moved up, he found himself doubting such people. Does this person know what matters? A pile of words also triggers suspicion; he cites the saying that VPs and directors can smell nonsense from five miles away and admits he would wonder what was being hidden or whether someone was dodging a mistake. Finally, there is cognitive overload: senior leaders run back-to-back meetings, sometimes double or triple booked. His reaction was to protect himself and ask whether someone else could present next time, which is how people lose their seat in decision-making meetings.

Source: Symptoms and perception

What to do instead

The mindset fix is that you have nothing to prove in this meeting. Mike recalls believing he had to impress a VP as if one meeting would earn a promotion; in reality reputation and artifacts build over time, and the pressure only made him lose his rhythm. The practical fix is brevity, more so as the room gets more senior, and speaking in terms senior leaders universally understand: time, money (revenue, cost, profit), customer happiness, which Amazon called customer delight, and opportunity. His working definition of a company is that it trades existing resources, money and time, to delight customers so it can gain more resources and future opportunity. Tie what you say to one or more of those.

Source: Less is more, and the universal currencies

How he practiced

Mike is not telling anyone to drop context entirely. Early in his career he rehearsed with his wife: if she got lost, a senior leader would too, so he cut words and refined the narrative. AI can help with this now, but he still recommends a human listener, because a face shows the moment someone gets lost or bored, which tells you where the redundancy or missing hook is.

Source: Rehearsing with a person

How authority is staged: the Milgram obedience study

A short explainer on Stanley Milgram's obedience experiments, showing that the setting and visible symbols of authority strongly shape whether people comply.

The clip retells the early-1960s experiments led by psychologist Stanley Milgram at Yale, inspired by the question of how ordinary people took part in atrocities under Nazi authority. Participants were assigned the role of teacher and told to give increasing electric shocks to a learner in another room, an actor who protested, begged, and eventually fell silent. When teachers hesitated, an experimenter in a lab coat used a fixed series of firm prompts, and the experiment ended only if all four were refused. The clip states that 65 percent of participants went to the maximum of 450 volts and that every participant reached at least 300 volts, many while visibly distressed.

Source: Setup and results

Factors the clip says changed obedience

  • Symbols of authority: obedience was high when the experimenter wore a lab coat and dropped sharply when an ordinary person gave the orders.
  • Location: obedience was high at Yale and much lower in a rundown office.
  • Proximity: the closer the teacher was to the learner, the less they obeyed.
  • Social support: when peers refused, most participants stopped too.
  • Conflicting commands: when two authority figures disagreed, almost no one continued.
Source: Variations

The clip also notes the heavy ethical criticism: deception, intense stress, an unclear right to withdraw, incomplete consent, and delayed debriefing. It adds that later replications under more ethical conditions and cross-cultural studies still found high obedience, and concludes that situational factors such as legitimacy, setting, peer pressure, and procedure powerfully shape choices.

Source: Criticism and replications
Editorial synthesis

How it connects to this book

The title promises that you can quickly establish authority, but the transcript stops at the study and gives no workplace instructions. The connection is editorial: the factors that raised obedience, visible legitimacy, setting, and social support, echo later advice about owning the meeting's framing, asking the question that steers the room, and bringing allies rather than escalating alone.

Section 09 · lesson

Why your brain freezes in meetings, and the speak-first rule

Mike explains the freeze through a simplified model of the brain under load and threat, then gives the habit that fixed his own you need to speak up feedback: always be the first to speak.

Many of us have heard you need to be more vocal in meetings or you need to speak up. Then in the meeting the heart races, you wonder what to say and which words to use, a topic you know finally comes up, and by the time you decide when to jump in it has passed. On the way home you replay the feedback and worry. Mike received similar feedback between roughly 2017 and 2019, while moving from middle manager to senior manager.

Source: The feedback and the freeze

The overloaded processor

His explanation, offered with the caveat that he is no neuroscientist, starts with the prefrontal cortex, which handles reasoning and decisions but burns a great deal of energy. Speaking requires three jobs at once: what to say, how to say it, and when to say it. For native speakers much of this is muscle memory handled by language areas he names as Broca's area, Wernicke's area, and the motor cortex, so they can think on their feet. Non-native speakers also have to retrieve grammar, pronunciation, and word choice deliberately, which maxes out the processor. He calls this cognitive overload.

Source: Three jobs and cognitive load

The alarm that makes you freeze

The worse part, he says, is the amygdala, which he compares to the grumpy old gatekeeper of a building complex who treats every child as a threat. It scans what we see, hear, and imagine, and when it senses danger it triggers stress hormones for fight, flight, or freeze. Humans are social, and for our ancestors being pushed out of the group could mean death. Not having a voice in the tribe's discussion still reads as that danger. So the gatekeeper keeps sounding the alarm, energy drains, and finally you freeze. That is why you can be exhausted after a meeting in which you said nothing, while your boss simply sees no contribution and no presence and repeats the label you need to speak up.

Source: The amygdala as gatekeeper

Mike's opening lines for speaking first

  • Large meeting, ten or more people: open with a quick win to set the tone. Before we kick off, I want to share a quick win from last week, or a quick update on something where we made a big stride. One sentence is enough.
  • Small meeting, three to five people: open with an alignment question. If you know the boss well: Joe, what do you want us to walk away with by the end of this meeting? If not: Before we dive in, is there an ideal target for this meeting that you want us to walk away with?
  • You are hosting and your boss is listening: I would like to align us on this goal first, state the goal, then ask: Any objections before I start?
Source: Scripts by meeting size

Mike describes himself as introverted. He made speaking first into a rule and practiced it until he no longer had to think about it. He connects this to Daniel Kahneman's Thinking, Fast and Slow, which he first read around 2017 or 2018: move habitual behavior into the fast system. Speaking first removes all three jobs at once, because what to say and how to say it are prepared and when to say it is decided. About six months later his boss told him he had really stepped up and that people spoke highly of his presence in different meetings. He frames the change as sending the gatekeeper home early rather than becoming a different person.

Source: Making it a habit

Four moves to hold the room when blame is in the air

In a compilation of four short lessons, Mike shows how a director reads public exchanges and gives scripts for being volunteered, asking for feedback, steering a fight, and facing an aggressive accusation.

Mike opens with a pattern from what he estimates are more than ten thousand hours of meetings: people rarely get blamed because they actually ruined something. It happens in the first few seconds of explaining themselves when challenged. Long explanations aimed at being understood push you lower each time and signal that you lack political savvy, so the other side marks you as a beginner and future blame flows your way. Explaining, he argues, is something the party in the wrong usually starts. East Asian upbringing and culture make self-justification instinctive, and if the other person wants a scapegoat, every extra sentence welds the blame on more firmly.

Source: Why explaining pushes you down ↗

Move 1: when you are volunteered in public

A colleague says in a cross-functional meeting: let's have Mike's team own this; you can get it done by Friday, right, Mike? As a senior manager and director, Mike often assigned work this way in front of a VP. It was rarely personal; he needed his leader to see that he had the problem covered and was showing leadership. What he watched was not whether you could do it but how you answered and how his VP's face reacted. If you say you have no bandwidth or hesitate, you become the scapegoat for unowned dirty work, and the assigner can lecture you about priorities, which shows off his leadership at your expense. Do not open with well, which reveals hesitation, disagreement, and low confidence in a single word. The question contains two questions: are you good with owning this, and can you finish by Friday? Answer in two parts: yep, totally on board with the direction; let me move things around and follow up with you offline on the timeline. Then stop. The pressure returns to the asker, and the timing moves to a conversation you control.

Source: Being volunteered ↗

Move 2: present the plan, then ask for objections

Asking a senior person what feedback do you have on this plan invites criticism. Mike admits that as a newly promoted director he felt he had to show judgment, so he would toss out have you thought about this, are you sure, or can we double-check, and each casual line became a day or two of work for you. Questions such as are you good with this or can I do this push the senior person toward finding fault. In a one-on-one, say instead: here is what I am going to do; we will do A because of B, and it will get us C. Any objections? Silence means consent, and the easiest reply to a leading question like that is no, I am good.

Source: Plan plus any objections ↗

Move 3: ask instead of asserting

In English-speaking workplaces, Mike says, the person with the right to ask questions is the leader of the meeting, and between peers everyone has that right without permission. Staying in the answering seat, even when right, invites attack: people find small holes in a flat statement and keep questioning until observers conclude you are the one in the wrong. When a cross-functional meeting turns into a fight, ask: hey guys, just want to take a step back. Are we solving the symptoms customers are seeing, or the root cause underneath? You give no answer, but you raise the level of the meeting and everyone follows your question. As a director he welcomed that person, because he could build on the question or give a ruling, and the boss concludes your thinking is at their level. His rule: if you are 60 to 70 percent sure of a view, the safest way to state it is as a question.

Source: The questioner leads ↗

Move 4: counter an aggressive accusation calmly

When a colleague aggressively blames you in front of your boss, for example asking whether the front-end data was your responsibility and whether you reconciled it properly, remember who the audience is. As a director Mike saw such clashes two or three times a week and remembered almost none of the details. What stayed was who panicked, who stayed steady, and who rushed to defend and talked more and more. Someone who barely engaged and only asked calm questions made the aggressive party look like the problem. Step one: acknowledge the emotion without accepting the accusation, and do not reflexively say sorry; say I hear your frustration. Step two: the accusation usually mixes what you knew with what you did not. Seize a detail you never heard about: can you elaborate on that? Who did you talk to on my team about this? People bluffing often reply well, I am raising it because it is urgent, and as a director Mike's judgment tilted against them at that word. Then say: this is the first time I have heard about this; is there a miscommunication somewhere? Their accusation becomes their communication gap. Finally hand the action back: I am happy to help; what is the fastest way you can get me the information or requirements I need?

Source: Steps one and two against blame ↗

Some people play less skillfully, raise their voice, and say something like I just want to call out that your team is blocking everything. Stay calm, lower your pitch, and repeat the last phrase back: we are blocking everything? What specifically makes you say that? The calmer you are, the weaker their case looks, especially with the boss watching. The goal is never to win the argument or force an admission. It is for your boss to leave the meeting knowing the problem is not yours, and from then on the boss tends to side with you.

Source: The loud accuser ↗

Choose who hears your questions

Bosses say no question is stupid, yet questions asked in the wrong forum shape how senior people judge you; who you ask and who is listening can matter more than the question.

Mike's conclusion comes first: the same question can help or hurt depending on whom you ask and who else is present. At the next morning's meeting the senior director was unhappy: there was too much back-and-forth to tell signal from noise. A chat with senior stakeholders should not be a forum for front-line questions, and every question effectively said look over here, my area is full of problems.

Source: The 2019 launch ↗

What Mike did

Mike offered that his team would own communication with the senior director: each data point would come with root cause and action items, in an accurate report every three hours, so the director no longer had to read the chat. Section 16 · lesson

Break a vague why-is-it-delayed question into parts

When a leader asks you a broad question about something you only partly own, answering it directly invites endless follow-ups; break it into components and hand each piece to its owner.

A common scene: in a meeting, a boss or stakeholder asks you about something that is not your responsibility. You worry that saying so looks like shirking and you do not know how to get the real owner to explain, so you say I will follow up. Something that was never yours becomes yours, and you spend hours of overtime on work that does nothing for your promotion because it looks like cleaning up your own mess. Mike says the deeper issue is not face but not knowing how to dismantle the other person's logic without a confrontation.

Source: The follow-up trap ↗

Why the obvious answer fails

Take why is this project delayed again, asked of you when you own only part of it. The asker is not necessarily malicious; the question is simply too broad. Complaining that the manager cannot see who is responsible does not help, because you cannot control what is asked. A typical answer is that a partner team had attrition last month and you hit some technical obstacles, which you are solving and will follow up on. It sounds fine but collapses under follow-ups: how do we know attrition was the issue, did no one step in, why was this not escalated? The more you explain, the more it sounds like excuses. Why delayed sounds goal-oriented but has no operational handle.

Source: Why direct answers fail ↗

The better answer

Move from the doer being held accountable to the leader helping everyone see the problem. If you own infrastructure development, say something like: happy to shed some light; let's take a quick step back and break down the components. First, infrastructure is about a week ahead of schedule and we are confident the trend is sustainable. Second, the pipeline build is on time. We did hit hiccups in UI and UX design and in the system algorithms, which created the bottleneck. Joe and David, I see you both on the call; can you fill us in on that side and our path to green?

Source: Breaking it down ↗

What that answer accomplishes

  • It turns one vague question into specific components.
  • It lightly clears your own part of the work.
  • It lays out, objectively, which parts have problems and who owns them.
  • It lets the owners explain for themselves instead of you judging them, shifting the meeting's and the boss's attention to them.
  • It saves the hours you would have spent aligning with other teams and writing follow-up emails, and it shows executive presence.

Question the premise before you catch the blame

A loaded question assigns you a role before you answer; instead of solving it, go upstream and test whether its assumptions are true.

Some colleagues pass blame through a tricky question. You end up saying I will follow up, work hours of overtime, and watch stakeholders credit the asker with leadership, sometimes even at promotion time. Mike's point is that the task is not to solve their problem or prove your technical ability, because starting to answer is the trap. Why do we keep delaying this feature request, we have talked about it numerous times, is not a request for a reason; it casts you as the person stalling the project. What actions are you going to take to solve this issue is not a request for your opinion; it casts you as the person who catches the problem. The real contest is over who defines the problem, because whoever defines it assigns roles and next steps.

Source: Questions that assign roles ↗

Go upstream

School trains us to jump straight into solving the word problem. At work your next move should be upstream: pull out the assumptions and premises and ask whether they hold. What assumptions have to be true for this question to be valid? Answering accepts all of them, and one may be the hole you fall into. His joke example is the classic chickens-and-rabbits puzzle, in which you count heads and feet to find how many of each animal are in a cage. Before writing equations, ask the person who set it why they can count heads but cannot tell a chicken from a rabbit.

Source: Go upstream ↗

Why this happens more as companies grow

If your company is small or growing fast, you may rarely see this, because growing the cake matters more than dividing it. Once an organization passes a certain size and resources become limited, friction over allocation produces negotiation, debate, and conflict. You can absorb some friction with your own time and energy, but that does not end the debate over the cake or guarantee you a bigger piece. Instead, others learn that your personal time can be sacrificed and use it as a bargaining chip, and your workload grows.

Source: Why this grows with scale ↗

Putting it into words

Replying I am tied up these few weeks, I will look into it, concedes the premise that you keep delaying, and your reputation erodes. Mike's alternative: I remember your request from our conversation earlier this week. Let's pause and let me clarify our intake process. A business unit submits a request through our ticketing system with clear requirements and impact, and every Monday our team reviews all new and existing requests to scope and prioritize them by impact and risk. When you say we have talked about it numerous times, was there a ticket your team submitted after our conversation? That is not an excuse; it puts three premises in front of everyone: why it has not been done, whether the request was formally submitted, and whether it is worth doing. The topic shifts from proving your innocence to whether their question deserves discussion, and over time people think twice before passing you blame.

Show your pressure gauge before the pot blows

Your manager is the cook using a pressure cooker without a gauge; report your workload while pressure is still manageable, and do it in ways that make adding more work visibly costly.

Mike pictures your job as a pressure cooker and your workload as its pressure. Too little pressure and the meat never cooks, which at work can mean being laid off. Most people only speak up when they are about to blow or already have, which forces the manager to rescue the work and the pot at once and creates strong negative feelings; a cook may stop using a pot that exploded. So report your workload and pressure while it is still manageable.

When the boss feels toxic and you cannot leave yet

Suppose your boss's tone drains you daily, but visa status or a thin network means you cannot leave soon. Mike starts by saying that judging a boss as toxic is subjective. Why reorgs happen

Mike gives two kinds of causes. External: shareholder expectations swing from revenue growth to profit to cost to new pillars such as AI, customer demand keeps rising, and the economy shifts, so growth is never linear. Internal: companies must keep hiring and promoting regardless, current structures move too slowly for new goals, big tech became top-heavy after years of exponential growth, front-loaded growth created duplicate work, and when a senior leader leaves, their scope is divided among others in a cascade down to individual contributors. Sam adds an example: when pandemic orders surged in 2020, by 300 to 500 percent in her recollection, Amazon's operations leadership reorganized to launch more buildings. Why a reorg can be an opportunity

  • Your boss faces uncertainty too and needs help.
  • Your new manager gains people or scope, and not everyone will like them; some will resign or transfer, and the vacant scope goes to the person the new manager trusts.
  • A new structure is drawn on a whiteboard to solve a problem no one has solved, and it inevitably has cracks. Leaders below the top must make it work, and early adopters who fill the gaps tend to be rewarded with scope or eventually a promotion. Mike likens it to a fast track.

Before the announcement: assess yourself

Because reorgs now arrive without warning, roughly yearly in big tech, Mike says to prepare continuously with a self-assessment. Do you have your manager's or skip-level manager's trust? Does your skip level see your work? Do you have solid stakeholder relationships, credibility, and reputation? The first 30 days

Mike's three steps after an announcement. First, send stakeholders and internal customers a personal message: you probably saw the announcement; restate what it says; I wanted you to hear it from me because I value our partnership; I will keep our communication clear; for now it is business as usual, and I will personally make sure your priorities stay intact and nothing falls through the cracks. A new manager spends the days after an announcement wondering who is upset, who will quit, what will break, and who will lead peers through the change. Friend, foe, or tool: greet people so you are not seen as a tool

Mike argues that people sort strangers into friend, foe, or tool, and that overcompensating to avoid looking like a foe makes many Asian professionals look like tools; he offers two mindsets and greeting habits to fix it.

The third bucket

Most Western advice on being social says people instantly judge whether a newcomer is friend or foe, so smile more, compliment more, and look less intimidating. Mike says it leaves out a third bucket: someone they can manipulate, a tool. Many Asian professionals fear being seen as a foe and overcompensate, smiling too much, complimenting too much, and nodding along even when they disagree. It feels safe, but it signals that we are neither friend nor rival, but someone who can be used. This need not be malicious; it is a split-second mental shortcut. He gives two causes: East Asian upbringing teaches people-pleasing, and we feel we need something from the other person, recognition, approval, or acknowledgment, especially from Westerners we perceive as having more power or resources.

Source: Friend, foe, tool

Two mindsets

  • Assume the other person has absolutely nothing to give you, instead of feeling you must please them or need something from them.
  • Knowing that, still make the conversation fun for both of you and enjoy the moment.
Source: Mindsets

Sending signals

Mike believes actions shape the mind, so he turns the mindsets into greeting habits. He states that about 70 percent of what we communicate is nonverbal, and that body language can carry only a few signals before it confuses people, so prioritize. For people who look like him, the top priority is signaling that you are not easy to manipulate. He points to a clip of Singapore's Lee Kuan Yew with Ronald Reagan at a White House reception: Lee stood still, mostly serious, laughed at Reagan's joke, then quickly returned to a serious face. Mike reads that as a signal of backbone from the leader of a much smaller country, and calls it the hardest part, without which you are not on level ground.

Source: Sending signals

Responding to signals

Balance it so you do not seem cold. Think of a good stand-up comedian, who rarely laughs at their own jokes but uses energy or wit to engage the audience. When asked how are you doing, I am good, how about you? signals either that you do not want to talk or that you lack confidence, which Mike calls a tool signal. Aim for a friend signal instead. Use energy, with phrases such as doing pretty awesome, beyond good, or living the dream, said with genuine energy rather than dread. Or use wit, with a beginner's formula: explain why you feel good with an absurdly related reason, such as feeling great because in two days you will not have to wake up early for the morning sync, or because your football team beat its rival in 2023. The gesture shows you are trying to make the moment fun, and the other person usually picks up the topic you chose.

QWhat is empire building, and why does it persist at large companies?

Ethan Evans

Ethan defines empire building as focusing on raw headcount rather than on what a team does or the impact it has. He says it exists because it is rewarded, and it is rewarded because counting people is the easiest thing to do. Impact is subjective: one leader may think a project was hard and another may think it was easy. Nobody can argue with the fact that one manager has 42 people and another has 17.

Ethan adds that some companies write headcount thresholds into the criteria for promotion. High performers given such a target will find a way to hit it, including by claiming they need people or by taking over other groups, and they will rationalize that to themselves.

Ethan says it is possible but takes more effort, because a manager's impact is fuzzy. A leader must decide what counts most: operational performance, features shipped, or whether those features make money. He gives the example of one product that directly earns revenue and another that is infrastructure keeping systems up. Comparing the two is hard.

Value judgments are also harder to defend than headcount. Telling someone that a peer was more impactful tends to make them angry unless they happen to agree. Ethan says that is why impact assessment is dangerous and gets done less than it should.

QHow can someone break out of the staff-engineer or front-line manager level where many people get stuck?

Ethan Evans

Ethan says this is his bread and butter. People get stuck because they keep doing the current skill harder rather than learning the skill of the next level. What gets someone promoted early is hard, effective delivery. Later they need to delegate, communicate and let go of hard skills, because micromanaging drives away good engineers. He points to Marshall Goldsmith's What Got You Here Won't Get You There.

His practical advice is to talk to people a level or two above and ask what they had to let go of and learn, and what they see holding the person back. He prefers asking for advice to asking for feedback, since feedback asks people to criticize and advice invites a more positive version of the same information. He sees many people working brutally hard but ineffectively.

If two equally qualified people on the same team want one promotion slot, how can one position first?
Ethan Evans

Ethan says the differences are relationship and trust. A senior leader has no time to micromanage and needs to hand work over and assume it will be handled well, not necessarily done the way they would do it. Trust, he says, is earned more in bad times than in good, like soldiers who fought together. At work the equivalent is a crisis such as an outage, a lost account or a big embarrassment, where someone shows dedication at a cost to themselves. The leader remembers who came through.

The second factor is whether the leader likes the person. Ethan says that if two people are equally qualified, a leader will spend more time with the one they enjoy, and that does not require kissing up but does reward being pleasant and helpful. He recalls hearing from Amazon leadership, he thinks Bezos, a test: when you enter a room, do you suck the oxygen out, or do people want you there? His rubric is whether people want you in the room.

Ethan acknowledges that engineers may dislike this and says he is trying to help, not to be liked. He is not apologizing for how the world works, and he wants people to know it.

QHow do you win champions for a senior promotion packet when you have no natural collaboration with the leaders you need?

Ethan Evans

Ethan's favorite method requires the manager to be on your side. As a manager, he would email peers or skip-level leaders to say that Ryan would be up for promotion in six months, that their group was adjacent, and that he would ask them for feedback. That gives them six months to watch, it does not feel self-serving since it focuses on their time, and six months later the leader cannot say they hardly saw the person. Ethan says he got good at getting people promoted, which helped him attract and keep talent; one person who missed a cycle gave him another six months, and the promotion came through.

If the manager is not on board, the next best option is for the person to approach the leaders and ask how to be in a natural position to give feedback over six months. Ethan advises oversubscribing, because of departures, reorgs and refusals: if you need four champions, ask six or seven. The ask can be framed as doing it properly and in advance, rather than as the usual last-minute request.

A busy vice president may see such a request as low priority. How do you raise it?
Ethan Evans

Ethan agrees it is low on their list, but says vice presidents also need other VPs to support their own promotions, so they know it is part of the system. To raise priority, find some relevance for them, answering what is in it for them, perhaps by showing how your work makes their life easier or faster. He says the system works largely because VPs understand it is the grease that keeps things flowing and can write approving feedback quickly.

For a manager who is supportive but passive, Ethan suggests coaching the manager, since the employee usually cares more about the career than the manager does. A middle ground is to ask the manager to send a short introduction asking the VP for a 15-minute meeting.

Can an engineer be promoted over the manager's objection?
Ethan Evans

Ethan says he has never seen anyone promoted over their manager's objection. A big boss who overrules a manager creates a mess, because the manager would then have to work with a more senior person they did not support. Engineers who imagine being recognized despite a poor manager, he says, will not be. They must win the manager over or move to a manager who is on board.

He describes making a profession of getting people promoted quickly and fairly, and says it became his recruiting tool. His best employee started as a level-five engineer and went through senior engineer, manager, senior manager and director in eight years of working together, until he left to found a company. QWhat can someone who despises politics do to avoid it?

Ethan Evans

Ethan gives two answers. First, choose a leader who shares the disdain, ideally from a similar technical background, and wants to reward the work. Second, work on the hardest area available, where expertise matters and the engineer stands out. He recalls Andy Jassy, then head of AWS, saying that a few principal engineers working on the hardest problems were relieved of the usual duties of reviewing architecture and mentoring because the company needed them solving those problems. Ethan notes that such engineers had the perfect boss.

He adds that a unique, valuable skill brings exceptions. When others complained that a high-level employee was not working at an Amazon level, Jassy said he agreed but that the person was in a role the company needed and knew it, so they would live with it. Ethan concludes that avoiding politics requires an essential skill and real leverage, which is the point of Cal Newport's So Good They Can't Ignore You.

Does a growing company have less politics?
Ethan Evans

Ethan believes so, and uses the post office as the extreme: roles are carrying mail or being postmaster, and there is only one postmaster until that person leaves. In a growing company, it is not twenty-five people wanting one promotion but many open opportunities. Over his 15 years at Amazon, he says, headcount grew from 14,000 to 1.4 million, a hundredfold. His first team had six people, and nine years later he led 800. He says that kind of growth opens doors that 5 or 10 percent annual growth does not, and his advice is to find high growth.

This source is not a video transcript. It is a set of dated meeting notes, January to March, from a reading group working through Camille Fournier's The Manager's Path chapter by chapter. Being managed: what the group drew from the opening chapters

  • Members' best managers understood the work and its constraints, communicated a clear vision and each person's role, avoided micromanaging, let people make mistakes with support, and shielded the team from demanding product owners and account managers.
  • Take responsibility for your own growth: know what you want, choose your manager, and interview management before joining.
  • Good mentors steer you to your own answers, challenge and support you, and are willing to say I do not know, let's work it out together.
Source: Management 101 · Mentoring

Managing people and teams

  • Start a reporting relationship by building trust, setting a 30-60-90-day plan, and communicating expectations; hold regular one-on-ones and keep shared notes.
  • Give light, regular feedback beyond good job so corrective feedback is accepted later; in reviews, cover the whole year with concrete examples and avoid big surprises.
  • The notes record the book's view that a performance improvement plan is often written so it cannot realistically be met, which makes it a gentler way of giving someone time to find another job.
  • When promoted over a peer: be open and mature, and pick your battles.
  • A manager acts as a shield, without treating the team like children: provide context and reduce gossip.
  • Handle conflict without relying only on consensus: depersonalize decisions through shared goals and risks, address simmering issues without drama, be kind rather than nice, and get curious.
